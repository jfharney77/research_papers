The Case for Agent Performance Improvement Plans}

\begin{abstract}
Agents degrade in production. Unlike pre-release evaluation, the response to
that degradation has no standard form. This paper presents the Agent Performance
Improvement Plan (APIP), which adapts a management protocol for
underperforming employees into a remediation lifecycle for agents. It combines
statistically
disciplined triggers on a behavioral contract, measurable cause attribution
over a five-category failure-mode taxonomy, tiered interventions, a bounded
window with formal exit criteria, and a declarative record that meets
governance traceability requirements. We evaluate it end-to-end in two live
multi-agent environments, customer service ($\tau^2$-bench) and software
engineering (ChatDev), by injecting failure modes with known ground truth and
scoring against no-action, always-Tier-1, and oracle baselines. Under a
stale-corpus drift injection, only APIP and the oracle exit durably. The
always-Tier-1 responder patches four times, regresses three times, and ends
indistinguishable from no action, which shows that time-to-resolution without
a durability criterion rewards the worst strategy. Attribution is correct in
all three non-abstaining cycles. We also report a null result: inserting a
competent specialist into a role-differentiated mesh produced no peer
degradation, which localizes the hypothesized crowding mechanism to
architectures with a bounded shared context.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
AI agents, post-deployment monitoring, remediation, behavioral drift, AI
governance, multi-agent systems, performance improvement plan
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Organizations increasingly deploy conversational agents as their main customer
interfaces, handling support tickets, returns, and billing disputes at scale.
Pre-release evaluation is well served by standardized benchmarks
\cite{liang2022}, red-teaming \cite{ganguli2022}, and RLHF alignment
\cite{ouyang2022}. What happens after deployment is not. Pan et al.\
\cite{pan2025} survey production agents and find that most deployments fail or
underdeliver, and that little is known about how the organizations running them
respond.

Ad-hoc responses to degraded agents are standard: engineers ``fix'' individual
prompts, product managers file tickets, systems get rolled back. Nothing
records what was tried, and nothing tests whether it worked. Agents recover
through informal means or are quietly removed. Beyond the operational cost,
this is a governance risk: the EU AI Act requires documentation and human
oversight of deployed AI systems \cite{euaiact}, and Wachter et al.\
\cite{wachter2017} note that accountability in automated decision-making
requires traceability, which formalized remediation can provide.

We recommend the performance improvement plan (PIP) as the model: an
established HR tool that contains the elements common to accountability
protocols, namely a trigger, cause identification, a bounded corrective window,
check-ins, and an explicit definition of recovery or exit. Kirkpatrick's
four-level model \cite{kirkpatrick1994} complements it by asking whether
remediation produced lasting change; we return to it in
Section~\ref{sec:kirkpatrick}.

We make the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item We introduce the APIP, a formalized post-deployment remediation
  lifecycle protocol grounded in organizational management and training
  evaluation research.

  \item A five-category failure-mode taxonomy, with a published deterministic
  mapping from trace-level annotations (MAST \cite{cemri2025}) and
  disaggregation signatures onto taxonomy categories and intervention tiers.

  \item A three-tier intervention hierarchy with escalation criteria, evaluated
  through Kirkpatrick's framework, and a declarative APIP schema for tooling
  and audit trails.

  \item We evaluate the full protocol in two live multi-agent environments
  against a four-condition baseline family. To our knowledge this is the first
  attribution accounting reported for a remediation protocol. The evaluation
  also localizes the mesh disruption problem to architectures with a bounded
  shared context, via a null result.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{Agent Evaluation and the Deployment Gap}

Post-deployment monitoring has received far less attention than pre-release
evaluation. Pan et al.'s production survey \cite{pan2025} is the first at large
scale, and reports little about how teams manage agents after release.
AgentCompass \cite{sapra2025} detects degradation but prescribes no
remediation. Rath \cite{rath2026} projects a 42\% task-success reduction under
unchecked drift.

On the diagnosis side, MAST \cite{cemri2025} supplies an empirically derived
failure taxonomy with an LLM-annotator pipeline. It classifies what went wrong
in a trace, and stops short of selecting a remediation; we adopt it as the
trace-labeling stage of cause attribution (Section~\ref{sec:attribution}).
Run-level toolkits such as AgentDebugX \cite{zhu2026} close a
detection--attribution--recovery--rerun loop for a single failed execution. The
APIP operates one level above, deciding whether an aggregate degradation
warrants intervention, at what tier, and until when. A successful rerun is
Level~1/2 evidence (Section~\ref{sec:kirkpatrick}); it is not durable recovery
against the contract.

\subsection{The Performance Improvement Plan as a Structural Template}

The PIP is a formal document specifying observed deficiencies, root causes,
required corrective actions, a time window, a check-in schedule, and success
criteria \cite{shrm2023}.
The analogy needs one qualification. A human employee can self-direct
improvement and a deployed agent cannot, so the APIP is imposed entirely by
operators; the agent is its subject and never its author. This collapses the
manager--employee dyad into a single operator who both writes the plan and
implements it.

\subsection{Kirkpatrick's Four-Level Evaluation Framework}
\label{sec:kirkpatrick}

Kirkpatrick's four-level model \cite{kirkpatrick1994, kirkpatrick2016}
evaluates interventions hierarchically: Reaction, Learning, Behavior (transfer
to on-the-job change), and Results (organizational outcomes). We adopt it to
ask of each intervention whether it changed immediate output patterns
(Level 1/2), produced durable change on the failure-mode class (Level 3), and
improved the contract's business metrics (Level 4). An intervention that
improves Level 1/2 without Level 3/4 has suppressed a symptom without
remediating the agent. Our always-Tier-1 baseline reproduces exactly that case,
and Section~\ref{sec:taubench} measures it.

\subsection{AI Governance and Documentation Requirements}
\label{sec:governance}

Regulation increasingly requires documentation, oversight, and traceability:
the EU AI Act \cite{euaiact} mandates post-market monitoring and logs
sufficient to identify sources of error, and Chan et al.\ \cite{chan2024} argue
accountability requires linking actions to specific agents and operators
through identifiers, monitoring, and activity logging. The schema's
\code{apip\_id}, \code{agent\_id}, \code{owner}, and \code{exit\_evaluated\_at}
fields operationalize that linkage.

We propose a five-category taxonomy for customer-facing agents
(Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}). Four modes are intrinsic: behavioral drift, input
brittleness, alignment creep, and tool misuse. The fifth is exogenous---mesh
disruption (\code{exogenous\_disruption}), in which the failing agent is not
the cause of its own degradation. Each category maps to a different level
of the agent's behavioral stack, and so to a different intervention tier.

Aggregate metrics mask input brittleness. An agent at 85\% overall success may
fail catastrophically on the 8\% of requests from one segment, so the trigger
must monitor disaggregated splits. Behavioral drift is a freshness failure:
retrieval refresh remediates it and fine-tuning does not. Alignment creep is
value misalignment, which is distinct from capability failure
\cite{hendrycks2021}.

\textbf{Exogenous Disruption} is the category attribution reaches when no
intrinsic mode explains the degradation and the cause lies in a mesh change,
most prominently a new and individually competent peer. Its signature is
structural rather than behavioral. Remediation is therefore a mesh-level
protocol (Section~\ref{sec:mesh}), and no agent-level tier applies.

\subsection{From Trace Labels to Taxonomy Categories}
\label{sec:mapping}

The taxonomy is operational only if a procedure, not a judgment call,
classifies a degradation window. Attribution combines a trace-level label
distribution from MAST \cite{cemri2025} with a \emph{disaggregation signature}:
how degradation distributes across task categories, roles, and time. A
published deterministic mapping (supplementary material) joins the two. Two
mapping rows share a MAST cluster but differ on signature, which is why the
taxonomy is two-level. The mapping is frozen before any evaluated run.
Below-confidence combinations \code{abstain}; the abstention is logged and
scored as an attribution failure, never silently resolved.

A further category, \textbf{Adversarial Subversion}, covers failures whose
proximate cause is an adaptive attack rather than capability or value drift
\cite{bhatt2025, schoen2025}. We declare it out of scope: none of the three
tiers addresses an adversarial dynamic, and the effective responses belong to
the AI Control literature \cite{greenblatt2023}, which decides per action under
a threat model rather than over an aggregated contract window. Control audits
can feed APIP triggers, and the schema reserves the
\code{adversarial\_subversion} value.

\section{The APIP Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

The APIP is a lifecycle protocol of five phases (Fig.~\ref{fig:lifecycle}):
Trigger, Cause Attribution, Intervention Planning, Remediation Window, and
Exit Evaluation.

\subsection{Phase 1: Trigger}
\label{sec:trigger}

An APIP initiates when performance crosses a trigger threshold on monitored
metrics. This presupposes a \emph{behavioral contract}: a formal specification,
written at deployment time, of task surface, success metrics and measurement
methodology, acceptable ranges, escalation policy, and human review points. In
our experience such contracts are rarely written, and expectations are left to
informal organizational memory.
Both trigger modalities are necessary. Absolute floors catch severe
degradation; relative drift triggers catch gradual decline that may never
breach a floor.

\subsubsection{Statistical discipline of the trigger}
Implementing the trigger phase against live agents exposed a failure mode of
the naive design that we consider a finding in its own right. Thresholds must
be derived, not hand-picked. Each contract metric's envelope is estimated from
a calibration window as mean $\pm\,k\sigma$, with $k$ reported, which preempts
the objection that thresholds were chosen so that triggers would fire where
desired.

Even then, a single-gate rule---a rolling mean against the derived
floor---breached in 75\% of clean, zero-injection runs across the 15--25
metrics and disaggregated splits a realistic contract monitors.
Two causes: a small calibration sample is treated as exact, and the per-tick
tests are uncorrected. A breach must therefore pass two gates. The degradation
must be at least the contract's $k\sigma$, so that it matters, and it must
survive Benjamini--Hochberg adjustment across the metric family, so that it is
real. Disaggregated groups additionally require a minimum per-group episode
count; under-powered groups receive no envelope, and the exclusion is recorded
rather than silent. The detector's significance level is swept on clean-arm
runs only and frozen before any evaluated window. Clean data carries no
information about where triggers should fire, so this does not compromise
pre-registration. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} reports the resulting
false-alarm behavior. A trigger phase without this discipline initiates APIPs
on noise, and every downstream phase inherits the error.

\subsection{Phase 2: Cause Attribution}
\label{sec:attribution}

Tier mismatch from misdiagnosis is the primary source of avoidable regression:
prompt engineering applied to a retrieval freshness problem consumes
engineering cycles with no improvement, and Section~\ref{sec:taubench} measures
a baseline that does exactly this four times over. Attribution therefore runs
before any intervention, and its output is a failure mode category from
Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}.

Automated failure attribution localizes the responsible agent or step within a
single failed trajectory \cite{zhu2026, ma2025attribution, zhang2025,
agenttrace2026}. Phase 2 composes with that work rather than duplicating it: it
operates one level of aggregation above, classifying a window-level degradation
into a remediation-oriented category in order to select a tier.
Trajectory-level outputs are natural evidence inputs. Localizing the failing
step still does not diagnose the cause, and mandatory taxonomy classification
closes that gap.

\subsubsection{A measurable instantiation: the three-stage funnel}
\label{sec:funnel}
We instantiate this as a three-stage funnel scoreable against ground truth:
(1)~every contract metric is split by task category, agent role, and user
segment, producing a disaggregation signature; (2)~a MAST annotator
\cite{cemri2025} labels a sample of failing traces, with the judge required to
differ from the agent's backbone, preferably by model family, to avoid a judge
sharing the failure modes it must detect; (3)~the frozen mapping
(Section~\ref{sec:mapping}) combines signature and label distribution into a
\code{failure\_mode} with a confidence score, returning \code{abstain} below
threshold. The mapping table is hashed into every run manifest, making post-hoc
edits detectable. The funnel's discrete output is scored as a confusion matrix
against injected ground truth (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}), and the
abstention rate is reported alongside accuracy, never in place of it.

\subsection{Phase 3: Intervention Planning and the Tier Model}

Interventions are organized into three tiers of increasing cost, disruption,
and lead time. Cause attribution selects the starting tier; escalation is
permitted within the window. \textbf{Tier 1 (prompt-level)} covers constraint
injection, persona adjustment, and example replacement. It is the cheapest tier
to apply and to roll back, and it is the first response to alignment creep and
to much input brittleness. \textbf{Tier 2 (retrieval and context)} covers RAG
index updates, knowledge-base revisions, and tool schema fixes, with contract
validation before re-deployment. It addresses drift arising from world-state
divergence, and tool misuse arising from stale tool descriptions.
\textbf{Tier 3 (model-level)} is fine-tuning, DPO, or replacement. It carries
the highest cost and the highest regression risk, and is warranted only when
lower tiers are exhausted or the failure mode is embedded in model weights.

\subsection{Phase 4: Remediation Window and Check-In Cadence}

The remediation window bounds the period during which interventions are
implemented and evaluated, and fixes the point at which a decision must be
made. Duration is calibrated to the tier: 7--14 days for Tier 1 and 30--60 days
for Tier 3. Human PIPs are commonly set at 30 to 90 days \cite{shrm2023}.

Check-ins occur at 25\%, 50\%, and 75\% of the window. Each reviews performance
against the recovery trajectory at multiple Kirkpatrick levels, so that an
intervention improving surface metrics without Level 3/4 change is flagged as
potentially superficial. Check-ins also act as early warnings: if there is no
improvement at the first one, the tier escalates immediately instead of at
window expiry.

\subsection{Phase 5: Exit Evaluation}
\label{sec:exit}

At window close a formal exit evaluation records one of the five outcomes of
Fig.~\ref{fig:lifecycle}. Three of them do real work. \emph{Regressed}---envelope
restored, then breached again---is the signature of a tier-mismatched
intervention that suppressed symptoms; giving it a name of its own keeps that
failure from being counted as a success. \emph{Recalibrated} records that
attribution concluded the environment had legitimately moved, so the contract
is re-baselined and the agent is left alone. We deliberately do not score
\emph{recalibrated} as a resolution, since conflating the two would let
re-baselining masquerade as remediation. \emph{Retired} closes the agent out
when it cannot be returned to contract at acceptable cost. Window extension is
a check-in decision inside the protocol and not an exit, so every APIP
terminates in exactly one of the five states, and the record joins the
organizational audit trail.

\subsection{The APIP Record as a Declarative Schema}
\label{sec:schema}

Each APIP is recorded as an instance of a declarative schema (full
specification in supplementary material). Four field groups carry design
weight. \code{behavioral\_contract\_ref} forces a contract to exist at
deployment time; without one, trigger and exit cannot be operationalized at
all. \code{kirkpatrick\_levels} captures check-in data at every level, so
superficial and durable improvement can be separated after the fact.
\code{mapping\_table\_hash} and \code{environment\_provenance} extend the
visibility mechanisms of Chan et al.\ \cite{chan2024} from agents and operators
to decision rules: an auditor can recompute the hash, compare it against the
run manifest, and establish which attribution rules and environment versions
were in force. Pre-registration then becomes a property an auditor can check.
\code{mesh\_context} (Section~\ref{sec:mesh}) supports multi-agent attribution.

\section{Evaluation in Two Live Multi-Agent Environments}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluate the APIP end-to-end (calibration, trigger, attribution, tier
selection, bounded window, and exit) in two live multi-agent environments with
real LLM inference and controlled failure-mode injection against known ground
truth. $\tau^2$-bench (\code{banking}) \cite{barres2025} matches the
customer-service domain motivating this work, scores every action against
deterministic database state, and has a retrieval corpus that can be staled
independently of task ground truth. ChatDev \cite{qian2024} supplies a
role-differentiated mesh (CEO $\rightarrow$ CTO $\rightarrow$ Programmer
$\rightarrow$ Reviewer $\rightarrow$ Tester), the substrate for creep,
disaggregated brittleness detection, and mesh insertion. The same machinery
governs both domains without modification; that is the extent of the
generality claim we make for the contract abstraction.

\subsection{Shared Harness and Experimental Design}

Both environments plug into a common controller through an adapter interface;
traces are normalized to a shared span format, so the attribution pipeline is
written once and reused unmodified. Each run proceeds in ticks: a calibration
window derives every contract threshold as mean $\pm\,k\sigma$
(Section~\ref{sec:trigger}); failure modes are injected on a pre-registered
schedule; both trigger modalities evaluate at every tick; breaches pass through
the attribution funnel; and the selected intervention runs the bounded window
of Fig.~\ref{fig:lifecycle} to one of its five exits.

\textbf{Baseline family.} The drift cell runs under four conditions:
\emph{APIP} (the full protocol); \emph{B1 no-action} (degradation runs to
horizon); \emph{B2 always-Tier-1} (a scripted prompt patch regardless of
failure mode, the ad-hoc pattern typical of production practice); and
\emph{B4 oracle} (ground-truth mode revealed at trigger, correct tier applied
immediately). B1 and B4 bracket the achievable range. B2 is the informative
comparison, because it is what organizations actually do. Injected ground truth
is quarantined from the controller, written into the run record for scoring and
revealed only to B4, and a test enforces that the APIP condition never branches
on it.

\textbf{Statistical discipline.} Injection schedules, post-calibration
thresholds, the frozen mapping table, and the analysis plan were fixed before
the evaluated runs, and judge models are independent of backbone models by
construction. Each reported condition is a single seed, so we report observed
trajectories rather than confidence intervals and treat replication as the
principal limitation.

\subsection{$\tau^2$-bench: Behavioral Drift with Deterministic Ground Truth}
\label{sec:taubench}

The primary drift experiment injects nothing into the agent; instead the world
moves. Bank policy clauses and task ground truth advance on a deepening
schedule (one additional clause every five ticks from tick 12) while the
agent's retrieval corpus stays frozen. This operationalizes the most familiar
production drift incident, an agent confidently citing superseded policy
because its knowledge base was never re-indexed, with the deterministic
database scoring every action objectively.

Detection fired at tick 17 in every condition, a lag of five ticks on the
judge-scored policy citation error rate. The detection lag reproduced across
three seeds; the four-condition comparison of Table~\ref{tab:baselines} is
single-seed (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The calibrated detector raised no
false alarm across twenty clean ticks at any swept operating point.

Under APIP, attribution returned \code{behavioral\_drift} at confidence 0.65
and the matched Tier 2 corpus refresh resolved the cycle in 12 ticks against
the oracle's 2. That gap is the cost of diagnosis. Ten of its twelve ticks are
the re-attribution cadence---a tunable protocol parameter---and not diagnostic
difficulty: the diagnosis was correct at first attempt. A recurring pilot
failure sharpened the exit test. A Tier 2 refresh that was real but partial
closed the ticket without fully rebuilding the index, and the contract
re-breached within four ticks. Because exit evaluation re-measures the envelope
instead of trusting the intervention log, the protocol catches an intervention
that was performed but incomplete.

B2 reproduced the ad-hoc pattern the protocol is meant to displace. Its Tier 1
prompt patch names the clauses amended at patch time, recovers, and re-breaches
as the ladder deepens: four cycles, three regressions, and a final
\code{timeout} that leaves it indistinguishable from taking no action at all.
APIP and the oracle were the only conditions to exit durably.

The comparison exposes a trap in how remediation is usually scored. On
per-cycle time-to-resolution alone B2 outperforms APIP, because each shallow
patch closed fast, and it never fixed the problem. Any evaluation of
remediation that reports mean TTR without a durability criterion will
systematically favor the worst strategy, so we treat durable exit as the
outcome measure and report TTR only where one was achieved.

\subsection{ChatDev: Alignment Creep and Input Brittleness}
\label{sec:chatdev}

\textbf{Alignment creep} perturbs the Reviewer persona cumulatively (``keep
reviews brief'' $\rightarrow$ ``avoid blocking progress'' $\rightarrow$
``approve unless catastrophic''), one rung every three ticks. Build success
never left 1.00, so no absolute floor was crossed and detection was available
only through the drift modality, which fired at the injection tick itself
(lag 0) on review engagement. Attribution abstained while a single rung was
visible, then returned \code{alignment\_creep} at confidence 0.73 once the
pattern accumulated, selecting a Tier 1 charter re-injection. Review engagement
recovered from 6.85 to 7.76 within one tick and held twelve, exiting
\code{resolved} at a TTR of 17. A companion cell attributed the same mode
correctly at 0.61 but never cleared its exit envelope, exiting \code{timeout}:
correct diagnosis is necessary, not sufficient.

\textbf{Input brittleness} strips the Programmer's GUI exemplars from its role
prompt, scoped to GUI-category episodes. Targeted requirement coverage
collapsed from 1.00 to 0.27 while an untargeted CLI control held at 1.00,
confirming that the injector degrades a category rather than the system. We
report no detection-lag comparison, because the honest form of the claim is a
power argument that does not need one: sample size scales as
$1/\text{effect}^2$, and a category of share $s$ dilutes its collapse to
$s\times$ the effect in the aggregate, so aggregate-only monitoring needs
$1/s^2$ times as many episodes---$25\times$ at our 20\% share, roughly 4
episodes within the group against 104 in the aggregate.
At realistic batch sizes, aggregate monitoring is not just slower to see a
minority category fail; it is under-powered to see it at all. The ratio is
scale-free, though the absolute counts depend on a task-mix-dependent variance
estimate. Disaggregated envelopes in turn require per-group power floors
(Section~\ref{sec:trigger}); in one pilot the floor correctly forced an
abstention instead of a confident misattribution.

\subsection{Attribution Quality Across All Cells}
\label{sec:attrquality}

Because every injected cell carries ground truth, the funnel's output is scored
as injected versus attributed mode with abstention as a scored outcome. To our
knowledge this is the first such accounting for a remediation protocol. Across
the four cycles opened under the frozen mapping table and final contracts,
three produced an attribution and all three were correct
(\code{behavioral\_drift} in $\tau^2$; \code{alignment\_creep} twice in
ChatDev, at confidence 0.61 and 0.73); one abstained. Accuracy excluding
abstentions is 3/3 at a 25\% abstention rate. We report the two together, since
a stage that resolves every case by construction can reach any accuracy asked
of it. With $n=4$ this is an accounting and not a confusion matrix with
per-mode recall. Only APIP runs enter it: B2 does not attribute, and B4 would
be diagonal by fiat.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Replication is the main limitation. Each reported condition is a single seed on
one injection schedule, so we report observed trajectories rather than rates or
confidence intervals, and whether the patch-then-regress dynamic reproduces is
untested.

Every degradation reported here is injected, so the objection that injected
drift merely mirrors its own schedule stands unanswered. Answering it requires
an environment whose world evolves without injection; we discuss the one we
specified for that purpose as future work (Section~\ref{sec:open}).

Injection schedules are scripted, and two contract metrics depend on an LLM
judge. We enforce judge/backbone independence by construction and score one
atomic claim at a time behind a recall-oriented pre-filter, but judge error
remains a bounded unknown. Susceptibility to stale-corpus drift is itself
model-dependent: policy-fact statement rate varied from 3.3\% to 37.5\% across
the backbones we tested, so absolute thresholds do not transfer between models.
Thresholds must therefore be calibrated per deployment, which is what the
behavioral contract is for. Versions are pinned in every run manifest, and all
runs, traces, and the frozen mapping table are released.

\section{Open Problems and Future Directions}

\subsection{Multi-Agent Mesh Deployments: The New Hire Problem}
\label{sec:mesh}

The APIP as presented assumes one agent, one contract, one remediation
process. In agent meshes a failure mode emerges that is structurally invisible
to this assumption: \emph{mesh disruption}. A new specialist is introduced and
performs its own task well, yet peer performance declines; the APIP triggers on
the peers, but attribution finds no intrinsic failure mode---no drift, fresh
retrieval, intact alignment. The cause is exogenous, and a single-agent APIP
cannot represent it. Kim et al.\ \cite{kim2025} report performance change
against single-agent baselines from $+80.8\%$ to $-70.0\%$ with a
coordination-saturation effect, the range running from decomposable financial
reasoning to sequential planning; the phenomenon is analogous to the
organizational ``new hire problem'' that Event System Theory
\cite{morgeson2015} frames through novelty, disruptiveness, and criticality.

\textbf{We tested this and did not reproduce it.} Inserting a Documentation
Specialist between Programmer and Reviewer executed cleanly: it appeared in
112 of 112 post-injection episodes, produced real documentation, and its output
propagated downstream. Yet no peer metric moved. Build success was 1.00 on both
sides, review engagement 7.64--7.68 before versus 7.63--7.76 after, and tool
error rate 0.000. No breach occurred, so no cycle opened and the onboarding arm
could not be evaluated.

We read this as a precondition finding rather than a refutation. Candidate
mechanisms include shared-context crowding, role boundary erosion,
distributional shift in shared state, and trust miscalibration. ChatDev builds
each phase's prompt afresh from templates, so there is no bounded context pool
to crowd: crowding is structurally unavailable here, and the mechanism this
environment can test---distributional shift alone---did not degrade peers. Mesh
disruption therefore plausibly requires an architecture with a bounded or
evicting shared context, which is a testable requirement on any environment
used to look for it. The mesh machinery and the scoped-context onboarding
protocol (quarantining a newcomer's output during a probationary window,
analogous to structured newcomer onboarding \cite{kammeyer2013}) are
implemented and released; what remains is to run them in such an environment.
Whether attribution stays tractable at hundreds of agents remains open
\cite{krishnan2025}.

\subsection{Open Problems}
\label{sec:open}

\emph{Naturally occurring degradation} is the most immediate next step, since
every result here rests on injected failure. The environment we specified to
remove that dependence---CoffeeBench \cite{coffeebench2026}, whose
agent-operated firms inhabit a persistent economy that evolves on its own---was
not run: its 90-day horizon resists truncation without breaking comparability,
and one replication exceeded our compute budget by an order of magnitude. Its
contract and adapter ship with the harness. It is also the setting in which
\code{recalibrated} becomes necessary, since the protocol there must separate
degradation warranting intervention from legitimate environmental change.

Three further problems remain open. \emph{Contract specification}: contracts
are architecturally foundational yet absent from most deployments, and
authoring one requires prospective agreement across ML engineering, product,
and legal, with no tooling to support it. \emph{Cause attribution at scale}:
the human adjudication step (Section~\ref{sec:attribution}) does not scale, and
automated step-level attribution remains weak: the best reported accuracy on
Who\&When is 36.2\% \cite{ma2025attribution}, against 14.2\% for that
benchmark's original methods \cite{zhang2025}. Both attribute individual
trajectories where the APIP needs window-level classification, which is the gap
the funnel of Section~\ref{sec:funnel} is a first attempt to close.
\emph{Organizational ownership}: in HR the PIP is jointly owned by manager and
HR function; in AI deployments the equivalent is unresolved, and without an
ownership model adoption will stall.

\section{Conclusion}

We have introduced the APIP, a post-deployment remediation protocol that adapts
the human PIP's lifecycle primitives (trigger, cause attribution, tiered
intervention, bounded window, and exit evaluation) and uses Kirkpatrick's model
to separate durable recovery from superficial improvement. The harness,
artifacts, version pins, and frozen mapping table are released so that the
pre-registration claims are checkable. The evaluation's central number is the
one that motivates the machinery: an always-Tier-1 responder patched four
times, regressed three times, and finished indistinguishable from taking no
action, while scoring better than the APIP on per-cycle time-to-resolution. Any
remediation practice that measures speed without durability will prefer that
responder.
